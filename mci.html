Maximum Credible Incident (MCI) Analysis

Calculating helium pressure losses along the burst-disc relief path during a Maximum Credible Incident.

The Problem

In an MCI event (maximum credible incident) such as a break in the beam tube or insulation vacuum, helium boils off and must vent through the burst-disk relief path.

The burst-disc must be sized according to standards. However, the current size ignored local pressure losses (losses due to bends, valves, and tees) along the relief path.

The image below shows a simplified diagram of the components in an RF cryomodule. The cryomodule contains four helium tanks, each housing RF cavities. These cavities are submerged in liquid helium, whose level is controlled by the bi-phase tube. The helium supply comes from the cryogenic distribution system. Before entering the cryomodule, the helium undergoes a final expansion through the Joule–Thomson valve. The return vapour from the cryomodule is also sent back to the cryogenic distribution lines.

During an MCI event, the helium in the cryomodule will almost instantly boil. The normal outlet to the distribution system cannot handle the resulting mass flow, so the helium is instead directed through the burst-disc relief line and into the tunnel. This is an event that should be avoided because of the loss of helium, the high pressures involved, and the potential damage to the cryomodule. No personnel are allowed in the tunnel during nominal operations, so the risk to human life during such an event is low.

Why Does It Matter?

  • Local losses mean the helium tanks see higher pressure than the burst discs during an MCI event.
  • This can push the cryomodule past its 2.05 bar limit and damage it.

Assumptions & Known Limits

  • Fluid properties are calculated using discharge properties, then held constant. No helium warming is considered.
  • Incompressible flow is considered.
  • Elevation / gravity is neglected.

Cryomodule Pressure Criteria

Criterion Pressure requirement
Nominal condition 1.3 bar / 4.5 K
Deformation limit Stay below 2.05 bar
Target total pressure drop ΔP (friction + local) < 10% of 2.05 bara = 0.205 bar*

*Comes from CRG-OP operational experience.

My Approach

  • Used helium-cryostat pressure-protection standards (EN 17527) to find the worst-case mass flow, which drives the ΔP.
  • Made a simple model in Excel to calculate the pressure losses along the path for various diameter sizes, burst-disc locations, and burst-disc counts.

This first sketch identifies the bi-phase tube inside the cryomodule and the nominal helium conditions used as the starting point for the pressure-relief assessment.

The second sketch shows the MCI break scenario: helium is released from the cryomodule into the relief line leading to the burst discs.

The final sketch combines the four helium tanks into one relief-flow model, showing how the total discharge travels through the pipes, fittings, and burst-disc line. I calculated the pressure loss along this path to find the true pressure differential the cryomodule will experience.

The Results

  • Pressure peaks farthest from the disc and is dominated (90-95%) by local losses.
  • Preliminary calculations show ΔP over 0.5 bar.
  • The current burst-disc is likely undersized.
  • Suggested design changes such as enlarging relief lines, smoothing out tees, and increasing the number of burst-discs lower the ΔP.
  • More dynamic modelling is needed.
